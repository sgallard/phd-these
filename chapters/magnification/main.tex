\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:magnification-overview}

Throughout the preceding chapters, the primary focus of this thesis has centered on addressing the operational and business challenges of modern publishing workflows. By establishing structure-aware template retrieval and multi-objective page layout generation, the methods developed in Chapters~\ref{chapter:template} and \ref{chapter:pagegen} significantly reduce manual editorial overhead and production costs within industrial press environments such as \melody. However, the utility of automated layout optimization extends beyond automation and business efficiency.

A key premise of this work is that the computational frameworks developed for layout synthesis can be repurposed to address critical accessibility and user-experience bottlenecks in digital media consumption. Specifically, the underlying re-layouting and optimization principles can be harnessed to automatically generate large-print newspaper layouts tailored for readers operating under constrained visual access scenarios. These scenarios encompass both device-related limitations, where small smartphone screens restrict the physical legibility of layout entry points for readers \NV, and reader-related limitations, where visual acuity impairments restrict access to layout structure even on larger displays for readers \LV. To demonstrate that automated layout adaptation for these scenarios represents a worthwhile and impactful application, this chapter presents a controlled empirical user study. By evaluating reading performance, spatial navigation strategies, and subjective workload, this study quantifies the tangible costs of standard gesture-based magnification compared to direct-access large-print layouts.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter set out to test the premise that the page generation machinery developed in the previous chapters could be repurposed beyond editorial production, to address accessibility bottlenecks in digital media consumption. The controlled study reported above provides empirical support for this premise. Under constrained visual access, \condtwonot was found to restore the behavioral integrity of the reading process: reading paths remained significantly closer to the natural, left-to-right and top-to-bottom strategy than under \condonenot, and participants formed a stronger mental representation of the page structure. This behavioral gain was accompanied by faster task completion and lower perceived workload. 

These findings indicate that manual magnification alone is insufficient to support effective reading under constrained visual access. The disruption caused by \condonenot does not stem from a lack of functionality in \panzoom, but from the fragmentation of spatial context it imposes on the reader. By contrast, re-layouting into \condtwonot preserves the spatial qualities of the page, in particular the direct legibility of entry points, and thereby sustains an efficient and predictable reading strategy. In conclusion, \condtwonot can therefore be regarded as an instance of inclusive design~\cite{langdon-inclusive-design-2014}.

%More broadly, this outcome can be regarded as an instance of inclusive design~\cite{langdon-inclusive-design-2014}.

These results were obtained on text-only newspaper pages; extending them to richer layouts is discussed as a direction for future work in \secref{sec:magnification-discussion}. The broader implications of repurposing the page generation methods for accessibility are revisited in Chapter~\ref{ref:conclusion}.
