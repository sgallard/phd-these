\section{The newspaper production context}

\subsection{The changing newspaper industry}
\label{sec:changing-newspaper-industry}

Historically, the press industry relied almost entirely on the physical distribution of print editions, with revenue coming from newsstand sales and print advertising~\cite{picard-free-distribution-2001}. The digital era gradually displaced this model, as advertisers moved their investment toward online platforms. 

This shift caused a structural decline in print revenue: by 2025, print circulation and advertising combined still represented 43.6\% of publishers' total revenue, while non-traditional ``other'' revenues, such as events, B2B services, and content-licensing deals with AI companies, nearly doubled their share of total revenue, from 13.2\% in 2021 to 25.4\% in 2025~\cite{radcliffe-wan-ifra-2025-2026}. Digital growth has not been enough to offset this decline (see \figref{ref:business-graphs}): between 2020 and 2026, publishers' combined print revenue is projected to fall from US\$68.7 billion to US\$50.1 billion, a loss of US\$18.6 billion, while combined digital revenue grows from only US\$17.5 billion to US\$23.8 billion, a gain of US\$6.3 billion; as a result, the industry's total revenue is expected to fall from US\$86.2 billion to US\$73.9 billion over the same period, a net loss of US\$12.3 billion~\cite{radcliffe-wan-ifra-2025-2026}. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\widthForOneImage]{img/plots.pdf}
   \caption{Global newspaper revenue, 2020--2026. Left: combined print, digital, and total industry revenue. Right: the four underlying revenue streams. Source: \protect\cite{radcliffe-wan-ifra-2025-2026}.}
   \label{ref:business-graphs}
\end{figure}

On the contrary, some publishers have managed to reverse their dependence on advertising through this same digital shift: \emph{The New York Times}, which drew roughly 80\% of its revenue from advertising less than a decade earlier, came to earn more than 60\% of its revenue from subscriptions~\cite{benson-paywalls-2019}.

Even so, this situation did not stabilize on publishers' own websites. Social networks then became the dominant channel for news consumption, further eroding direct web traffic as readers favored aggregated, algorithmically curated feeds over visiting individual newspaper websites. More recently, generative artificial intelligence has introduced a further threat to this already fragile digital revenue base. AI-generated answer summaries placed directly within search engine results, such as Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode, reduce the incentive for users to click through to the source article~\cite{lefigaro-apig-ai-overviews-2026}. In France, this led the Alliance de la Presse d'Information Générale (APIG), which represents more than 300 publications, to refer the matter to the French competition authority in 2026, arguing that the feature had been deployed without a fair and transparent negotiation over neighboring rights~\cite{lefigaro-apig-ai-overviews-2026}. Publishers expect search-referred traffic to fall by 43\% over the following three years~\cite{lefigaro-apig-ai-overviews-2026}, a decline that threatens both advertising and subscription revenues, since a shrinking online audience weakens both at once. This confirms that the whole press industry, not only its print branch, faces mounting economic pressure under the current technological landscape.

\subsection{The role of print in newspapers}

Despite crisis across both print and digital channels, the prints remains worth to produce, for two reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item The print edition is frequently positioned as a premium offering, in comparison to the digital: industry reporting describes the printed newspaper as adapting to an audience demand for curated, collectible experiences that stand in direct contrast to the digital infinite scroll, framing it as an alternative to an increasingly online reading experience~\cite{radcliffe-wan-ifra-2025-2026}. This premium positioning is further reinforced by the digital and AI-driven disruption described above: publishers are increasingly looking for differentiated products whose value cannot be replicated by generative AI systems that summarize or repurpose commoditized web content, and the print edition, with its distinctive two-dimensional layout, is precisely such an alternative. This suggests that a broader ``revival of print'' may become a defining trend of the coming years. A quote from a real publisher supports this claim:

    \begin{quote}
    "{\itshape The link between audience engagement and good design has never been stronger, and it's becoming ever more crucial to commercial success in the digital age}",
    from~\cite{Mor16}
       \label{quote:design-engagement}
    \end{quote}

    \item The value of the newspaper layout extends far beyond physical paper. Its enduring relevance rests on proven cognitive and navigational advantages~\cite{ozretic_dosen_key_2018, hollander_e-reader_2011, holsanova_entry_2006}. Unlike linear digital feeds, layout-based media distribute information across multiple spatially distinct blocks, giving readers structural awareness and a global overview of the content. Entry points such as headlines, subheadings, and visual cues guide attention, foster serendipity, and shape content consumption in ways unique to these documents~\citemine{gallardo-imx}. Simply linearizing articles for responsive digital screens discards this two-dimensional spatial organization. The challenge for the industry, therefore, is not to abandon the layout, but to translate its two-dimensional richness into physical and digital formats alike, without degrading the reading experience. Quotes from real readers support this claims:
    
    \begin{quote}
    "{\itshape I couldn't get the big picture of anything}" \\
    "{\itshape I really like being surprised by [...] finding an article that I wouldn't normally gravitate to, but because of the picture or something, you read it, and you kind of feel enriched because it's something that you wouldn't ordinarily read}",
    readers reacting to an e-reader edition stripped of the print edition's editorial hierarchy~\cite{hollander_e-reader_2011}
    \label{quote:global-awareness}
    \end{quote}

   
 
\end{enumerate}



\begin{callout}[Print as a two-dimensional layout, independent of its physical medium.]
More generally, what matters is not the physical paper itself but the two-dimensional layout in which news content is spatially arranged. This has an important corollary: since the layout paradigm is not tied to a physical medium, it can equally be instantiated digitally. Dynamic, multimodal editions that keep the full richness of digital features, such as embedded video, interactivity, and personalization, while preserving the navigational benefits of the print layout, are therefore a compelling use case. \textbf{Accordingly, throughout this thesis, the term \emph{print} is used to denote either a physical print layout or its digital two-dimensional equivalent, unless stated otherwise.}
\end{callout}

\subsection{From editorial content to printed pages}

This economic pressure did not only reshape business models: it also transformed the daily operational paradigm of newsroom production. 

In the pre-digital era, print journalism was historically tied to the physical page format. Under this traditional ``layout-driven'' approach~\cite{digitizing-news}, journalists wrote articles directly into predefined layouts, bound by the physical space of the printed sheet (\figref{fig:layout-driven-vs-content-driven}a). This spatial constraint limited the length of each article and shaped the writing process around fixed geometric containers, but it also meant that layout composition happened at the same time as content creation.

Digital media shifted newsrooms toward a ``content-driven'' workflow instead, as shown in \figref{fig:layout-driven-vs-content-driven}b. In modern multi-platform newsrooms, editorial content (text, headlines, and multimedia assets) is created independently of its final geometric representation. Journalists now write under a ``web-first'' paradigm, producing stories for digital platforms with no initial layout boundaries. This shift increased publication speed and article volume, but it also created a major challenge for layout-based formats~\cite{newspaper_design_book, newman2024}: unstructured web content must now be fitted, after the fact, into structured two-dimensional layouts for print and digital layout-based editions.

Fitting web articles into structured print canvases is a significant operational burden for modern newsrooms. Because content no longer carries built-in geometric boundaries, editorial teams depend on Content Management Systems (CMS) to format, organize, and assemble it into publication-ready pages~\cite{newspaper_design_book, wagner_trends_2017}. This transition from layout-driven to content-driven workflows defines the environment in which modern CMS platforms operate, and sets the stage for evaluating the industrial solution studied in this thesis.

\section{Newspaper production at \melody}

\subsection{The company and its activities}

The CMS studied throughout this thesis to address this transition is \melody, developed by Demain Un Autre Jour (DAJ), a French software publisher specializing in digital technologies for the creation, structuring, transformation, and multi-channel distribution of editorial content~\cite{melody}. Led by CEO Bruno Génuit, an industry expert with over 25 years of experience in press digitalization and editorial workflow management (including prior leadership roles at Ouest-France and IFRA)~\cite{melody}, the company provides software solutions to press organizations, publishing houses, and media groups. 

The company's flagship product, \melody, was conceived as an intuitive, human-centered Content Management System (CMS) specifically engineered for journalists, freelancers, and newsroom operators. DAJ's primary mission is to streamline the editorial value chain, enabling media organizations to store, reference, and manage their complete information assets, including text streams, photographs, audio, and video, without volume limitations across physical and digital channels~\cite{melody}.

\subsection{The Melody platform}

Operating as a web-native Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) platform, \melody provides a collaborative environment accessible through standard web browsers. Currently deployed across more than 150 newsrooms of varying scales, including major french digital deployments such as \emph{Actu.fr}, the software manages the end-to-end editorial chain from content creation to final publication. The platform's industrial reliability is reflected in its high satisfaction rate of over 95\%~\cite{melody}. 

From an architectural perspective, \melody relies on automated transformation engines that convert native layout formats (such as Adobe InDesign) and legacy digitized archives into fully structured, semantically tagged XML documents. This structured XML foundation enables the logical decoupling of raw editorial content from its geometric layout representation, facilitating multi-channel (web and print) editing across web and mobile platforms. Thanks to this architecture, \textbf{\melody has the ability to bridge digital and physical workflows, enabling editors to generate both web-responsive articles and print-ready newspaper pages through an user-friendly web-to-print workflow}, reducing pre-press production time by half compared to earlier, fully manual workflows, according to Bruno Genuit.



\subsection{The current web-to-print workflow}

\subsubsection{Basic elements}
\label{sec:basic-elements}
To formally characterize this manual workflow, it is necessary to first identify the fundamental structural elements manipulated within the \melody environment. To bridge unstructured digital news articles with structured physical  pages, the system relies on four core elements: the \emph{Page}, the \emph{Web Article}, the \emph{Template}, and the resulting \emph{Print Article} (See \figref{fig:page-article-template}). The following provides operational definitions of these elements, which correspond to their formal treatment in Chapter~\ref{chapter:definitions}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\widthForOneImage]{img/page-article-template.pdf}
    \caption{Problem input entities: (a) Page canvas, (b) web article, (c) template, and (d) print article.}
    \label{fig:page-article-template}    
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Page.} Represents the global geometric container or physical canvas upon which articles are arranged. Industrially, a page is defined by its sheet boundaries, specifically its width  and height, along with outer margins that delineate the effective printable area, as shown in \figref{fig:page-article-template}(a). Within the \melody environment, the page canvas acts as a completely free-form spatial container. While visual grid indicators are available to assist operators during manual layout, the canvas area itself remains unconstrained, serving as the physical two-dimensional space where multiple articles, advertisements, and visual assets must be allocate (\figref{fig:melody_manual_a}).

\paragraph{Web article.} Represents a web article authored by journalists. Operating under a ``content-based'' paradigm, web articles exist purely as digital content without layout topology. Within the web-to-print workflow, each web article is defined by key metadata attributes extracted directly from \melody database which provides its meta-characteristics to produce a print version afterwards (\figref{fig:page-article-template}b): body text character count, total number of associated images, Boolean flags indicating headline or subtitle presence, and an editorial hierarchy flag designating whether the item serves as the main lead story or a secondary piece.

\paragraph{Template.} A \emph{Template} acts as the architectural skeleton or pre-designed layout topology that converts a web article into a multi-column print article. Selected from an extensive catalog $\templatecatalog$ within \melody, a template dictates the internal spatial organization, topology, and geometric zoning of headlines, body text columns, photo placeholders, captions, etc. (\figref{fig:page-article-template}c). 

\paragraph{Print article}
A \emph{print article} represents the concrete instantiation that results from assigning a specific template to a web article to be displayed in the print.



\subsubsection{Web-to-print workflow}

\melody implements the ``web-to-print'' workflow shown in \figref{fig:web-print-workflow}, following two main sequential steps, starting from the input which are web articles (See demonstration video: \mytt{https://youtu.be/rTQozZUWEWU}):

\begin{enumerate}    
    \item \textbf{Template assignment (Step 1):} To convert a  web article into a two-dimensional layout component, an operator must select first an appropriate design template  from an extensive catalog  within \melody. This selections binds the text and multimedia assets into the template's pre-defined zoning topology, instantiating a print article  with explicit topological characteristics.
    
    \item \textbf{Print layout building (Step 2):} Once individual articles are formatted into print components, the operator moves to the page building phase. In this step, the print articles must be manually allocated and positioned onto the  page canvas. The operator manually adjusts spatial arrangements to ensure that all elements fit within the printable bounds without overlapping, while meeting the visual quality expected from the publication. Within this process, one article per page is naturally singled out as the main story, reflecting its editorial hierarchy and prominence relative to the other articles on the same page.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations of the current workflow}
\label{sec:limitations-current-workflow}

However, while \melody provides a robust web-native environment for web and print content management, bridging the gap from  web articles to print pages has historically relied on labor-intensive, semi-manual processes (\figref{fig:melody_manual_a} and \figref{fig:melody_manual_b}). Although the system offers web-based editing tools that allow operators to format web articles into its print format (\figref{fig:melody_manual_a}), and drag-and-drop articles into the page canvas to produce print layouts directly within a browser interface (\figref{fig:melody_manual_b}), \textbf{the workflow lacks automated decision-support mechanisms}. Consequently, operators must manually produce each page, an arduous task that required more than one hour of manual editing per page before the introduction of \melody's web-native tooling, and that still requires an average of fifteen to twenty minutes per page today; aggregated across a full edition of many pages, this individually modest duration still accumulates into several hours of manual editing.

More specifically, in terms of the workflow:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Step~1 requires human operators to manually search through large template catalogs containing hundreds of highly similar options to find a compatible fit for each article. 
    \item  Step~2 obliges operators to perform complex manual spatial reasoning such as dragging, dropping, and adjusting layout blocks across free-form canvases to achieve a high quality layout without exceeding page constraints, a bottleneck that increases production costs.
\end{enumerate}

Taken together, these limitations show that the operational bottleneck does not stem from a lack of digital tooling within \melody, but from the absence of decision-support mechanisms capable of automating template selection and page composition. Closing this gap therefore requires moving beyond incremental improvements to the existing web-based editing tools, toward computational methods able to perform these tasks autonomously. The following section motivates this shift from both an industrial and a societal perspective, before \secref{sec:scientific-challenges-contributions} translates it into the concrete scientific challenges and contributions addressed by this thesis.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

\textbf{This thesis argues that the print production workflow can be automatized  by introducing intelligent, computational frameworks}. This matters for two reasons: industrially, automation directly relieves the economic pressure of the press industry by cutting the cost of manual page composition; societally, the same spatial-layout techniques can be repurposed to serve readers with visual limitations.

\subsection{Industrial motivation}
\label{sec:industrial-motivation}

From a strategic perspective, page composition time has already been substantially reduced by the introduction of \melody's web-native tooling, from more than one hour of fully manual editing per page to an average of fifteen to twenty minutes per page with the current semi-manual workflow. Automatizing this remaining workflow would further accelerate page composition to a few minutes of fully automated execution per page, all while strictly preserving the visual quality of the publication. This tolerance of a few minutes per page is specified by \melody as the operational requirement for automated composition, since page building is not a real-time task but must fit within the daily publication cycle. Beyond this immediate cost objective, eliminating the remaining production time also opens a longer-term industrial perspective: on-demand, near real-time newspaper production, which neither \melody or any other CMS currently have.

From an operational perspective, automating layout generation fundamentally transforms the workflow. By delegating repetitive template selection and geometric fitting to computational engines, editors and layout designers are freed from mechanical constraints. Human expertise can thus be reallocated toward high-level creative curation, investigative journalism, and visual storytelling. Crucially, this efficiency is achieved without compromising layout quality, thereby protecting brand identity and reader engagement of the print.

\subsection{Societal motivation}
\label{sec:societal-motivation}

% Accessibility of printed/digital newspaper content; ageing population;
% visual impairment; access to information; large-print needs.

\textbf{Beyond its economic and industrial impact, this thesis explores a secondary application that addresses a societal challenge: digital reading accessibility for individuals under constrained visual access (related to the device or the individual itself)}. This thesis aims to demonstrate that the same foundational techniques developed for industrial web-to-print automation can be repurposed to synthesize accessible, ``large-print'' digital layouts, that is, layouts enlarged for readability without altering their original two-dimensional structure. By automatically enlarging typographical entry points (e.g., headlines and section headers), the proposed framework delivers direct-access reading experiences that restore visual scannability, significantly improve reading performance, and reduce perceived cognitive workload. This approach targets readers with moderately degraded vision, who retain the ability to benefit from, and are accustomed to, a spatial layout; for readers with severe low vision, for whom layout-based reading is not feasible a priori, linear alternatives combining large, well-spaced text with audio, such as Vocale Presse~\cite{vocalepresse}, remain more appropriate.

\section{Scientific challenges and contributions}
\label{sec:scientific-challenges-contributions}

Automating this workflow, and repurposing it for accessibility, unfolds along three axes: retrieving an appropriate template for each article, composing a complete page from a set of web articles under multiple competing objectives, and quantifying the impact of the large-print layouts enabled by this workflow for readers under constrained visual access. Each of the following sections motivates its axis, states the scientific challenge it raises, and introduces the corresponding objective and contribution of this thesis.

\subsection{Learned template retrieval}
\label{sec:learned-template-retrieval}

Within Step~1 of the \melody workflow (\secref{sec:limitations-current-workflow}), assigning an article to an appropriate template first requires characterizing what distinguishes one template from another within an extensive catalog $\templatecatalog$, often containing hundreds of  options. Beyond this geometric characterization, automated selection must also reflect the tacit editorial preferences historically encoded in the operator's own choices, factors such as visual balance and newspaper style that are not directly observable from a template's  topology alone. Retrieving a suitable candidate set therefore requires both a notion of structural similarity between templates that generalizes past historical usage frequency, and a mechanism able to capture this tacit editorial style.

Accordingly, this thesis aims to \textbf{quantify the increase in structural diversity and long-tail coverage that topology-aware template retrieval achieves, in a single-article context, with respect to usage-driven and topologically-blind baselines, at a bounded loss of conformity to the historical template selections}. 
Chapter~\ref{chapter:template} addresses this objective through the Template Retrieval System (\trs), which relies on a template-based application of a Graph Matching Network ($\gmn{\cdot}{\cdot}$) to measure pairwise structural similarity and drive a diversity-aware clustering and retrieval framework. 
\textbf{The chapter further extends this similarity-based scoring from the single-article setting to a page-aware context through \neuralmethod, a neural scorer trained on \melody's historical assignments that captures the tacit editorial preference introduced above}, and that is reused as one of the competing objectives optimized in Chapter~\ref{chapter:pagegen} (\secref{sec:multi-objective-page-generation}).

\subsection{Multi-objective page generation}
\label{sec:multi-objective-page-generation}

Selecting a template for each article in isolation, however, does not by itself produce a complete page: Step~2 of the \melody workflow (\secref{sec:limitations-current-workflow}) additionally requires arranging every print article within the page canvas without exceeding its printable bounds, and it is this step that accounts for most of the time currently required. Producing a full page couples a discrete template-assignment decision for every article with a two-dimensional spatial packing problem, a combination whose search space grows exponentially with the number of articles, so that exact global optimization becomes computationally intractable in general~\cite{lodi-packing-survey}. 

Page quality cannot be reduced to a single scalar criterion either: it results from several, partly conflicting measures, such as spatial occupation, adherence to editorial style, text-capacity fit, and positioning, each answering to a different concern of the production process, such that improving one measure comes at the expense of another. 

This thesis therefore aims, as its main objective, to \textbf{automate the \melody web-to-print workflow, generating a complete print page from a set of web articles without manual template assignment nor manual placement, in a few minutes per page against the fifteen to twenty minutes per page currently required, and with objective values within the ranges observed in operator-produced pages of the same publisher}. 

Building on the page-aware template-selection scoring introduced in \secref{sec:learned-template-retrieval}, this thesis further aims to \textbf{quantify the improvement in trade-offs among the quality measures that the proposed multi-objective method, which incorporates this scoring as one of its optimization objectives, attains over constructive and ablated baselines}. 

Chapter~\ref{chapter:pagegen} addresses both objectives through \methodNameGeneral, an evolutionary framework built on NSGA-III~\cite{nsga-iii-pt1, nsga-iii-pt2} and its preference-aware variant R-NSGA-III~\cite{rnsga-iii}, in which editorial preference is expressed as a target point in the objective space rather than as a fixed constraint, biasing the search through reference directions generated locally around that point.

\subsection{Large-print adaptation}
\label{sec:large-print-adaptation}

Beyond industrial production, \secref{sec:societal-motivation} argued that the same developed techniques can serve readers under constrained visual access by synthesizing large-print editions. Automatically enlarging a layout's font size while preserving its underlying two-dimensional organization, however, raises a distinct challenge: gesture-based magnification such as pinch-and-zoom is the established alternative, with well-documented benefits but also known costs, such as a loss of the reader's global awareness of the layout, whereas this direct-access large-print alternative remains comparatively unstudied. Whether it actually performs better in terms of reading time, navigational pattern, and perceived workload is therefore an open question.

This thesis therefore aims to \textbf{validate that large-print layouts reduce reading time and perceived workload, and better preserve the behavioral integrity of the reading, with respect to gesture-based magnification, for readers \NV\ under device-related visual constraints (e.g., small mobile screens) and readers \LV}. 

Chapter~\ref{chapter:magnification} addresses this objective by repurposing the developed techniques to generate large-print editions, and reports a controlled user study comparing reading performance, navigational cost, and perceived workload between direct-access large-print layouts and gesture-based magnification.
