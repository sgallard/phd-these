Why Print Is Poised for a Comeback in the Digital Age
While much is being discussed about the possibilities and power of artificial intelligence, Ludovic Martin presented a different and compelling thesis at the Online Print Summit in Munich.

By Redaktion

6 min. readView original
While much is being discussed about the possibilities and power of artificial intelligence, Ludovic Martin presented a different and compelling thesis at the Online Print Summit in Munich. According to the French print consultant, print is on the verge of a comeback. His argument: digital fatigue, AI-driven opacity and changing purchasing processes are creating new space for haptic communication. For online printers, this represents an opportunity – but only if they rethink their business model.

Print as a response to digital overload

“Print counter attacks” – even the title of Ludovic Martin’s presentation was designed to provoke. After all, the industry has been facing very different challenges for years: declining volumes, budget shifts into digital channels and growing price pressure. Martin did not fundamentally question this development, but argued that the environment is changing. In his presentation, he traced the development from the euphoria of the early internet years through social media, the pandemic and the AI boom to a counter-movement: instead of “freedom” and the “shifting of old boundaries”, terms such as fear, dependency, loss of privacy, AI slop and “digital burnout” are now increasingly dominating public discourse.

The starting point for his thesis was an observation: a young Gen Z project team member had a cassette lying on his desk; not an old one from the 1990s, but the latest Taylor Swift album. He said that in this format, he could listen to music with greater focus. Martin interpreted this not as a love of retro, but as a signal of a broader trend back toward analogue media. His thesis: digital natives in particular are rediscovering analogue, monofunctional devices – as a counter-model to the permanent state of alert created by the smartphone.

Why haptics are becoming relevant again

From this observation, Martin derived a broader movement. For many young target groups, print is not simply an old channel, but a medium with characteristics that are difficult to replace digitally: no push notifications, no tracking, no constant interference with attention through advertising overlays. Print does not need to be loaded, can be passed on without rights restrictions and is calm in the best sense of the word – and therefore, in its own way, “respectful”. In an over-digitalised world, that is precisely what could make it attractive again.

There is also a security aspect. Ludovic Martin referred to emergency plans in hospitals, where printed documents serve as a final safeguard in the event of IT failures or cyberattacks. This is less about nostalgia than about a functional argument: what is not connected cannot be hacked. At a time when resilience is once again being discussed more widely, print is gaining a new legitimacy.

Haptic marketing instead of scroll logic

In his keynote, the French print consultant also linked this idea to marketing. If consumers are once again looking more strongly for tangible, trustworthy experiences, then this demand will also change brand communication. He spoke of “tangible marketing” – communication that does not merely generate clicks, but can be physically experienced. For brands that risk becoming invisible in an AI-dominated search and shopping environment, print could therefore regain strategic importance.

The idea behind this is simple: if AI assistants in (agentic) commerce only make a few brands visible, many providers lose reach before the actual selection process even begins. The counterargument from the French industry expert is therefore not “back to the past”, but rather: new visibility through other channels. Direct marketing, postal inserts, haptic brand moments or attention-grabbing print mailings could become a powerful instrument for greater competitiveness in this environment.

A warning to the printing industry

However, anyone who now believes that this development will automatically benefit print service providers and drive their growth is mistaken. Ludovic Martin warned the OPS participants not to leave their own business models unquestioned and unchanged. The core business model of many providers is already 25 years old and was built for “version 1 of e-commerce”. If print becomes attractive again, it may in fact be new players who benefit – platforms, software providers or start-ups that integrate print into new customer journeys.

He cited Canva as an example. There, print is no longer the starting point of the journey, but merely an output at the end of a design, campaign or brand management process. Those who order at this point often no longer compare offers with traditional print portals. Platforms such as Canva think print seamlessly into the process – and that is precisely what is shifting the rules of the market and fundamentally changing the graphic ecosystem.

This is particularly attractive for younger target groups because they are growing up with such tools and use them as a matter of course. Martin sees the mistake more with established print providers, who justify price increases with AI instead of creating low-threshold models. So if print service providers want to benefit from the comeback of print, they must rethink their own business models.

Visibility also comes through storytelling

Martin did not describe the path into this “new print world” in technical terms. Instead, he called for better storytelling. On platforms such as TikTok or Instagram, for example, videos of cutting machines or insights into production processes perform surprisingly well. People want to understand how things are made. For printing companies, this offers a real opportunity to make technologies, materials, finishes and applications visible – not as a parade of machines, but as a narrative about impact, quality and experience.

This creates a shift in perspective: away from pure product sales and toward the “translation” of print into relevance for new target groups.

From print provider to integrator

In Martin’s view, online printers must also redefine their role. In future, it will no longer be enough to sell price, product and quantity. What will be needed are providers that integrate print into existing customer processes – into CRM systems, marketing automation, ERP landscapes and, in future, agentic commerce models as well. Printing companies must understand their customers’ customer journeys, analyse touchpoints with their customers’ customers and derive concrete print applications from them.

His image of the “print integrator” is likely to have touched a nerve with some providers. Many of them still argue from a production perspective. Martin, by contrast, spoke about interfaces, data models, standards, API logic and new purchasing behaviour. He expects today’s webshop to lose importance as the dominant model, while headless commerce structures, chat interfaces and other dialogic access points gain ground. This is another reason why the industry must address compliance, system openness and AI readability.

What online printers can learn from this

Four concrete tasks can be derived from Martin’s presentation:

Redefine target groups: not only buyers and marketing managers, but also app builders, developers and new commerce intermediaries
Re-explain print: through storytelling, KPIs and haptic impact
Open up systems: plugins, integrations and standardised interfaces will become more important
Understand customer processes: those who only sell products will become interchangeable; those who integrate applications will gain relevance
A pointed thesis with a serious core

One may consider Ludovic Martin’s forecast that print will experience a comeback in the coming years to be bold. But the underlying point is sound: the digital logic of the past two decades is no longer unassailable. Generating attention is becoming more expensive, achieving digital visibility more difficult, and customer trust anything but guaranteed. It is precisely in this field of tension that print can regain strategic value – not as a mass-market channel, but as a deliberately deployed, haptic and integrated communication medium.
